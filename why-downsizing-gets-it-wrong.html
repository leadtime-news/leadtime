The Word Worth Retiring: Why Downsizing Gets It Wrong

Inaccurate words send people into big decisions looking over their shoulder instead of ahead.

By Karen Light  ·  July 27, 2026

There is a word that follows people through their sixties and seventies and eighties, and it arrives long before the movers do. The word is downsizing. It shows up in conversations with adult children, in brochures for retirement communities, in the gentle suggestions of friends who have already made a move. It sounds practical. It sounds sensible. And for a word meant to describe one of the most considered decisions a person makes, it does a surprising amount of quiet damage.

Consider what downsizing actually says. It says “less”. Less space, less stuff, less of the life that filled the larger house. It frames the whole undertaking as subtraction, as a reduction of something that used to be fuller. A person hears downsizing and pictures what they are giving up, rarely what they are moving toward. That is a strange way to describe a decision that, done well, is mostly about gain.

The move is not always about square footage. It is about a home that matches the life being lived now, rather than the life that was lived twenty years ago. The four-bedroom house made sense when there were four bedrooms’ worth of people in it. When the people have grown and gone, the house has not shrunk to fit. It has simply become a home designed for a chapter already lived, which is a different problem than having too much space. It is a home that no longer answers the questions its owners are actually asking.

Those questions tend to be sensible ones:

  • Where do I want to be when the stairs stop being convenient?
  • What would it feel like to spend less of a Saturday maintaining a property and more of it doing something else?
  • Which of these rooms have I actually walked into this month?

None of that is about becoming smaller. All of it is about fit.

This is why the better word is planning. Planning points forward. It assumes a person is making a choice rather than accepting a loss, and it puts them in the position they should be in: the one making the decision. A person who is planning is deciding what the next home needs to do for them. A person who is downsizing is being told, however kindly, that they have too much and should have less.

The distinction is not only emotional, though the emotional part is real. It is also practical, because the words we use shape the timeline we keep. Downsizing sounds like something that happens to you, eventually, probably later. Planning sounds like something you do, on purpose, while there is still room to do it thoughtfully.

The families who fare best are almost always the ones who treated the move as a plan rather than an event that finally forced their hand. They looked at the house early. They asked what it was doing for them and what it was costing them, in time and in energy and in the low background hum of maintenance that a large property never really turns off.

There is a version of this decision that gets made in a hurry, after a fall or a diagnosis or a season that made the stairs feel longer than they used to. And there is a version that gets made with a year or two of runway, over unhurried conversations, with time to sort what matters from what merely accumulated.

The second version is almost always the better one, and the only thing separating it from the first is that someone decided to start before they had to. The version that starts early begins with a shift in how the whole thing is framed. This is the same idea that runs underneath my article, When The House Stops Fitting. The house has not changed; the life inside it has, and naming that honestly is where the good decisions begin.

Language does real work here. Call it downsizing, and the task feels like managing a decline. Call it planning, and it becomes what it actually is: a person deciding, with clear eyes and enough time, where and how they want to live next. The house itself is not always the point. The point is a life that fits the person living it, and there is nothing small about arranging for that.

So perhaps the word “downsizing” is worth retiring. Not because it is unkind, but because it is inaccurate, and inaccurate words send people into big decisions looking over their shoulder instead of ahead. The more honest word available is “planning”, and it happens to be the more useful one too.

Starting that conversation is its own difficulty, and one both sides tend to leave to the other. I wrote about that in The Conversation Everyone Is Waiting for Someone Else to Start.
