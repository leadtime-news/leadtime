When the House Stops Fitting

Same house, new questions, and some time to think about the answers.

By Karen Light  ·  June 9, 2026  ·  Revised November 25, 2026

Across North America, the largest generation in modern history is moving through its 60s, 70s, and 80s. It is a stage of life where every housing decision carries real weight, and most people facing those decisions want the same thing: to stay in the home they already have.

When researchers ask 55+ homeowners whether they want to remain in their current home as they age, the answer comes back the same way across surveys, regions, and income brackets. The vast majority want to stay put. Aging in place is not a niche preference. It is what most people plan for, even when they have not put the plan into words yet.

There is a catch, though, and it is worth naming early. Most of the homes people want to grow old in were not designed with that long stretch of life in mind. The house was built for the family that bought it, at the age they were when they bought it. The years move forward. The house stays exactly where it is.

A house can fit for thirty years and then stop

A home can suit you perfectly for three decades and then, almost without your noticing, stop suiting you.

The stairs that were nothing in your fifties become something to plan around in your seventies. The bathroom that worked for a younger body does not accommodate a walker. The basement laundry, the second-floor bedroom, and the long hallway down to it. None of it changed. The person using it did.

People rarely notice the shift while it is underway. They notice it once, in a single moment. A missed step. A longer-than-usual rest at the top of the stairs. A grandchild or a friend who points out something everyone else has been working around for months.

The house did not stop fitting all at once. It stopped fitting in pieces, and one day the pieces added up.

The questions that follow are gentle, but they stay

Once you see it, a few questions tend to come to mind. They are not alarming questions. They are the practical kind, and they do not go away on their own.

  • What does this room actually need to do now that it did not have to do before?
  • Which parts of the home are still working, and which parts are we just working around?
  • Are the people who matter most still close enough?

That last one matters more than people expect, because the decision that follows is rarely about the house alone. It is about the neighbourhood and the people in it. It is about how close family lives. It is about the cost of staying measured against the cost of moving, and about what home will mean in the next chapter of life.

These are not questions to rush. They are questions to sit with while there is still room to choose calmly, rather than under pressure later on.

This is the work of the thinking-ahead years

There is a long window, often years wide, between the first time someone notices the house has changed and the day an actual decision gets made. That window is where the good thinking happens.

Used well, those years let you make small adjustments before they become urgent ones. A room can be reconsidered for what it needs to do now. A layout can be eased. A plan can take shape gradually, at a pace that feels like yours rather than someone else's.

For the family helping, too

If you are the adult child in this picture, much of the above will sound familiar from the other side. You may be the one who noticed the missed step. You may already be helping, or sensing that you will be before long.

The same gentle questions apply to you, with one addition: how do you raise any of this without making a parent feel like they're being managed? The honest answer is that it goes better when it starts early, as a series of unhurried conversations rather than one difficult talk after something has gone wrong. Having ample lead time ahead of any urgent situation is as much a gift to you as to them.

A note from the editor

Planning for housing later in life is a subject I have carried around for a long while. I have watched it happen in my own family and in the families I worked with as a REALTOR® and Seniors Real Estate Specialist® in Calgary.

There is the slow shift from a house that fits to a house that does not, and the moment someone finally says it out loud. The questions in this piece are the ones I hear most often, sometimes years before any decision gets made.

There is also the question of what to call any of this. I wrote about that separately, in The Word Worth Retiring.

This Lead Time website and my Lead Time newsletter exist for those years. They are the thinking-ahead years, before the deciding-now years arrive. They are the planning-ahead years, with plenty of lead time.
