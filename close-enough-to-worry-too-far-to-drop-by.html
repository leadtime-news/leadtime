Distance

Close Enough to Worry, Too Far to Drop By

Distance changes what helping looks like. It doesn’t change how much it counts.

By Karen Light  ·  November 25, 2026

There is a particular kind of worry that comes with helping a parent from another city. It shows up when the phone rings at an odd hour. It shows up when a call goes unanswered for longer than usual. And it shows up every time a parent says, “Everything is fine“, in a voice that doesn’t quite match the words.

A parent in another city is close enough to worry about and too far away to drop by. Distance does not stop anyone from helping. It changes which kind of help is possible.

What travels well

A surprising amount of helping can be done from anywhere with a phone. Appointments can be booked. Groceries can be ordered for delivery. A refill can be requested from the pharmacy. A call at the same time on the same day each week can become something both people look forward to.

None of this works well when it is done to a parent rather than with one. A parent who is still running their own life does not need an assistant in another province making decisions for them. They may well welcome one who makes the phone calls they would rather not make.

What does not travel

Some things need someone at the door. A light bulb in a ceiling fixture needs a ladder and a steady pair of hands. A medical appointment needs a ride there and someone to sit in the waiting room. A smoke alarm that starts chirping in the middle of the night needs a new battery that night, not next month.

Then there is the noticing that only happens in person: the fridge that is emptier than usual, the mail piling up on the counter, the stairs that are taking a little longer to climb. None of that comes through on a phone call.

That is where the people nearby come in. It might be a neighbour with a spare key, a friend down the street or a sibling who is a 20-minute drive away. Many families have someone like this, even if nobody has ever said out loud that they are part of the arrangement.

The near sibling and the far sibling

When siblings are spread out, the work tends to split unevenly. The sibling who is closest does the driving, the drop-ins and the dealing with whatever broke this week. The sibling who is farthest away often feels guilty, a little useless and a step behind on the news.

Both of those are tiring. They are just tiring in different ways. The one nearby can start to feel like the only one doing anything. The one far away can start to feel like the last to know. Neither feeling is anyone’s fault. It is simply what distance does to a family.

The visit that turned into a work trip

Then there is the visit itself. It is planned weeks ahead and it costs a plane ticket or a long drive. By the time it arrives, the list of things to fix has grown. There is a burnt-out bulb, a dripping tap, a new TV remote nobody has figured out and a stack of mail waiting on the kitchen table.

By the end of the visit, the tap has stopped dripping. Sometimes nobody has sat down for a proper cup of coffee.

The fixing matters. But many parents will remember the afternoon someone took them out for lunch long after they have forgotten who changed the bulb.

What makes distance easier

Distance is hardest when the information is missing. Which pharmacy fills the prescriptions? Who is the family doctor? Who has the spare key, and what is the neighbour’s phone number? Who services the appliances? From another city, each of those questions can turn a five-minute call into an afternoon of phone calls.

When the answers are written down and kept where the right people can find them, much of long-distance helping becomes a short phone call.

Nobody can do everything from a plane ride away, and nobody is expected to. With the right information and the right people nearby, a great deal can be done from wherever you are, including the part that matters most, which is staying close.
